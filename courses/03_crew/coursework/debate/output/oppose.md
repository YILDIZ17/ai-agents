I oppose the motion.

**Strict laws to regulate LLMs would do more harm than good** because they would freeze a fast-moving technology before its benefits can fully reach society. LLMs are already improving education, accessibility, productivity, coding, research, and customer support. Heavy-handed regulation would not just slow “bad actors” — it would also slow hospitals, teachers, startups, and ordinary users who depend on these tools to work faster and better.

The core problem is that **strict regulation is often too blunt**. LLMs are not one single product; they are a general-purpose technology used in thousands of ways. A rigid legal framework can easily become outdated, overbroad, or impossible to enforce. By the time lawmakers catch up, the technology has changed. That creates a system where only large corporations with legal teams can comply, while smaller innovators are locked out. In practice, strict laws can **entrench the biggest companies** rather than restrain them.

We already have better tools than sweeping regulation: existing laws against fraud, defamation, copyright abuse, identity theft, and harmful business practices. If someone uses an LLM to scam, harass, or deceive, punish the misuse — don’t burden the entire field with restrictive rules. **Targeted enforcement is smarter than blanket regulation.**

Most importantly, innovation depends on experimentation. If every new model must pass through heavy legal barriers, progress slows, costs rise, and the public loses out on life-changing advances. The answer to risk is not suffocating control; it is **responsible development, transparency, and case-by-case enforcement**.

So the choice is not “safety or chaos.” The choice is between **smart, flexible oversight** and **strict laws that choke innovation and protect incumbents**. For that reason, the motion should be rejected.
